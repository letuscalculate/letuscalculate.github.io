Two students are working on a group project. They don’t talk about it, but they both end up doing the same thing. Each of them pastes the reading into a language model, and then sends the other one whatever it said. They put their conclusion together from those two answers, they both agree with it, and the whole thing takes maybe ten minutes.

And nothing about this seems strange. People have always relied on things they can’t check for themselves. But it’s worth taking a closer look at what just happened, because this is quickly becoming a normal way to work.

Here’s one way to picture it. Think of everything you believe about some question as text written inside you. Some of it is pretty tentative: maybe, it depends, I’m not sure yet. Every time an answer comes in from outside, a bit more of that space fills up with someone else’s sentences. Nobody’s forcing anything. You read the answer, it sounds reasonable, so you keep it. And after enough of these, the space is full, and it’s hard to say which parts you actually wrote yourself.

Let’s make this concrete. Say the students’ presentation ends with this sentence: remote work makes people lonelier. They both agree with it. But what if someone asked each of them, "Is this your judgment?" It’s not obvious what they should say.

That’s really the question of this video. When a person and a language model reach a judgment together, how much of the reasoning does the person need to have themselves?

### Taking someone’s word for it

There’s a philosopher who thought about this long before language models. In 1985, John Hardwig wrote a paper called "Epistemic Dependence." He starts by admitting something: he believes lots of things he has no evidence for. That smoking causes lung cancer, for example. And that’s true for pretty much all of us. Most of what we believe, we’ve never checked.

So what do we have instead? Hardwig says we have a different kind of reason. It’s not evidence that the claim is true. It’s a reason to think the person telling us has the evidence. Hardwig keeps these two apart, and it helps to do the same with our sentence.

Imagine two boxes. The first one is for reasons behind the claim itself. Which studies looked at loneliness? How did they measure it? Who did they compare? Are there people for whom it goes the other way? In our example, this box is empty. Neither student looked at any of that.

The second box is for reasons to trust the source. Here there’s at least something. The model is usually right. It sounded confident. And two different models gave the same answer. Those are the things that made the sentence feel safe to accept.

But are these good reasons? According to Hardwig, trusting someone makes sense when you have good reason to think they’ve actually done the work, the inquiry, needed to reach the conclusion. So let’s go through them one by one.

"It’s usually right." That’s about a track record. But usually right about what? Neither student knows how often it gets questions like this one right.

"It sounded confident." That’s about tone. And something can sound completely sure and still be wrong.

"Two models agreed." This one sounds the most like a real check, because it’s like asking a second source. But two models trained on mostly the same text aren’t really independent, the way two separate witnesses are. So it might be more like asking the same witness twice.

Each of these makes the sentence easier to accept. But none of them actually shows that anyone did the inquiry.

### Nobody holds the whole chain

So should we just never rely on things we can’t check? Hardwig actually argues almost the opposite. In his words, "rationality sometimes consists in refusing to think for oneself."

His example is a physics paper from 1983 with ninety-nine authors. One of the authors explained to him how the work was split up. Some people built the equipment, others collected the data, and separate groups analyzed it. No one person could have checked the whole thing. Many of the authors probably couldn’t say how a particular number in the paper was worked out. And yet, Hardwig says, it would be strange to claim the result isn’t known.

So knowledge never really worked by everyone holding all the reasons. The reasons are spread out, across people, records and instruments. What makes it trustworthy isn’t that everyone checks everything. It’s that every link can be checked by somebody. There are lab records you can look at, results someone else can try to repeat, and named authors you can ask, who are responsible if it’s wrong. Hardwig also points out that the chain has to end somewhere, with whoever actually has the evidence.

That gives us a better way to ask our question. Instead of asking whether a link is a person or a machine, we can ask: can this link be questioned? If something’s wrong, who would notice, and how would it get fixed?

And when one of the links is a language model, those questions get harder. Often there’s no record of what the answer was based on, nobody who actually did the inquiry, and no simple way to ask why. It’s not clear where the chain ends.

### After the presentation

Let’s see what difference this makes. Go back to our sentence, and imagine three people who all end up putting it in a presentation.

Person A just copies what the model said.

Person B asks one more question: why? The model explains. The explanation is clear and well organized, it makes sense, so B goes with it.

Person C goes and looks at the evidence. Let’s say, just as a hypothetical, that C finds the effect shows up mostly in people who live alone and just started a new job, and it’s much weaker for people who chose to work from home and have a social life outside work. So C changes the sentence: for some people, in some situations, remote work is linked to more loneliness.

Now imagine the Q&A after each presentation. The teacher asks the same thing every time: why do you think that, and are there counterexamples?

A doesn’t have anything to add.

B can repeat the explanation. But one more question, like, how did they measure loneliness? And B gets stuck. The psychologists Rozenblit and Keil found that people usually think they understand how things work much better than they really do, until you ask them to explain it step by step. They called it the illusion of explanatory depth. And it’s easy to imagine a smooth explanation making that illusion even stronger.

C can answer. And just as important, C can tell you when the sentence stops being true.

So all three said almost the same thing. But their relationship to that sentence is completely different. A has cues. B has an explanation, but it came from the same place as the claim. C has evidence, and that’s what lets C notice a mistake and change their mind.

Hardwig’s point still stands. Not everyone has to be C. A, B and C can all be part of a perfectly good chain. The problem is a chain where nobody is C.

So, back to our two students. If they’re more like A or B, that’s not necessarily a problem, as long as someone further down the chain did what C did. The tricky part is that with a language model in the chain, it’s hard to tell if anyone did.

And this might get harder. Right now the answer shows up on a screen, so at least we can see the moment it comes from outside. If one day it shows up more directly, say through a brain-computer interface, even that moment might be hard to notice.

If you couldn’t tell which thoughts came from a model, would they still be yours?
